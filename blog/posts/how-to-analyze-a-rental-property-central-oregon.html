How to Analyze a Rental Property in Central Oregon

By Christian Lisignoli  ·  September 8, 2026  ·  4 min read

When I look at a potential rental property, I'm trying to answer one question as fast as possible: does the math work? If it doesn't, I move on. If it does, I dig in deeper. Here's exactly how I do it.

Step 1 — Add Up Your Monthly Costs

Start with what you'll owe and spend every month. There are four buckets:

Your Monthly Cost Buckets

PITI — Principal, Interest, Taxes, Insurance. This is your mortgage payment plus property taxes and insurance. Run your numbers through a mortgage calculator with your expected loan amount and current rates.

Utilities — If you're covering any utilities (water, trash, gas), include them here. Many landlords pass these to tenants — confirm what's typical for the property type and area.

Property Management — If you're not self-managing, budget 9–10% of gross rent. Even if you plan to manage it yourself, run the numbers with this included. It stress-tests the deal and accounts for the day you may not want to anymore.

CapEx Reserve — Set aside 5–10% of gross rent each month for big-ticket repairs: roof, HVAC, water heater, appliances. These will come eventually. Budget for them now so they don't wreck you later.

Add all four together. That's your total monthly outgoing.

Step 2 — Find Out What It Will Rent For

You need a realistic rent number — not a hopeful one. Here's how to find it:

Start with apps. Search active rental listings for comparable properties nearby on Zillow Rentals, Apartments.com, Facebook Marketplace, and Craigslist. You're looking for similar size, condition, and location.

Then check Zillow's sold/rented comps. On Zillow, filter for recently rented homes — not just active listings. Active listings are asking prices. Rented comps are what the market actually paid.

Call a local property manager. This is the fastest shortcut. Tell them the address, the size, and the condition and they'll give you a range in two minutes. They rent homes every week — they know.

Use all three sources to narrow down a confident rent range, then underwrite to the low end of that range.

Step 3 — Do the Math

This part is simple:

Monthly Rent − Monthly Costs = Surplus or Deficit

If you have a surplus — even a small one — that's a deal worth looking at harder. Positive cash flow on a straight purchase in today's Central Oregon market is genuinely rare, so when it shows up, pay attention.

If you have a deficit, that doesn't automatically kill the deal. It tells you the numbers don't work at this price. Which leads to the next step.

Step 4 — Back Into a Price That Works for You

If the deal doesn't cash flow at the listing price, flip the question: what purchase price would make it work?

Start with your rent estimate and your required monthly surplus, then work backwards through your costs to find the loan amount — and therefore the purchase price — that gets you there.

That's your number. Offer accordingly. Sometimes you'll be close to asking price. Sometimes you'll be far enough apart that the deal just doesn't make sense. Either way, you'll know exactly why — and you won't be guessing.
